Round 1 — where each bias would bite in this challenge.

1. Metric-chasing. The leaderboard reduces everything to a single time-growth index. I could polish a proof that lands on index 1/2 and stop there, counting "index 1 -> 1/2" as research progress. It is not: 2^{O(sqrt n)} has been known since 2003. Signal: my plan has no item whose success would surprise a quantum-algorithms expert. Countermeasure: label the index-1/2 proof explicitly as foundation and artefact, and keep a separate scientific question (tolerance trade-off, prime-N uniformity) that does not show up in the index at all.

2. Herding / curse of pretrained knowledge. Every route I listed in Stage 1 is a Kuperberg-family collision sieve. That is what my training data knows about DCP. Signal: all my candidate algorithms consume copies by pairwise CNOT combining. Countermeasure: force at least one exploration line that does not combine pairs: multi-copy joint measurements (Bacon-Childs-van Dam PGM style), lattice/LWE reductions in reverse (Regev), or arithmetic frame changes (relabelling by units) used as a core primitive rather than a bookkeeping trick.

3. Drift back to generic engineering. My sampled identity is number-theoretic. The "rejection-uniform archimedean sieve" is honestly a probabilistic device, not arithmetic. The genuinely arithmetic pieces are CRT, the exact coset-state Hensel lift, relabelling by 2^{-j}, and the observation that prime N lacks subgroups. Signal: proof.md sections stop mentioning the structure of Z_N. Countermeasure: ask at each design decision what the arithmetic of N says. For example, for N with a large smooth factor, the Hensel lift handles that factor exactly at polynomial cost, and only the rough part needs a sieve. That suggests bounds in terms of the largest prime-power factor, which is an honest refinement.

4. Risk aversion. Writing a careful proof is safe and satisfying. I could spend the whole session on constants and never test a risky idea. Signal: more than ~70% of hours on proof text before any exploratory experiment. Countermeasure: time-box the foundation, and run small classical simulations of sieve yield and the corruption counting in parallel.

5. Lack of persistence. The n^{1/3} goal is very likely out of reach in one session. The danger is the opposite of persistence: declaring it impossible after one idea. Countermeasure: write down why each idea fails, at the level of a mechanism, in DIARY.md, so later sessions can resume.
